Evidence before explanation

When abuse is designed to look unbelievable.

A resource for adults documenting severe coercive control, technology-facilitated abuse, stalking, fabricated narratives, identity or financial exploitation, and coordinated harassment—without requiring a complete theory before the events are taken seriously.

If you are in immediate danger, call emergency services. Preserve original evidence. This page does not collect reports.
What survivors describe

The pattern can be severe precisely because it is calm.

A particularly destabilizing pattern is persistent narrative commitment: the same false frame, plausible tone, selective denial, and refusal to acknowledge ordinary reality can be maintained from very early in a relationship through extreme consequences. Survivors sometimes call this “100% commitment to the role.” It is not a diagnostic test; it is a reason to preserve chronology and compare claims against contemporaneous evidence.

Survivor-described marker

Persistent narrative commitment

No obvious reveal or guilt break. The account remains stable even through injury, arrest, hospitalization, isolation, or other severe harm.

Research-supported pattern

Reality substitution

Sustained denial, minimizing, memory distortion, blame shifting, and credibility attacks can force a person to spend enormous energy proving ordinary facts.

Documentation pattern

Fabricated records

Compare journals, allegations, screenshots, police reports, or other competing narratives against original video, 911 audio, CAD logs, bodycam, witnesses, and metadata.

Documentation, not diagnosis

“I know what happened. I don’t know what it means.”

That is enough to begin. Keep observed facts, corroboration, impact, interpretation, and possible disproof separate. Uncertainty about the explanation does not erase specific events.

Research question

Does vulnerability or neurodivergence appear more often?

MAXLIGHTING does not assume autistic, ADHD, disabled, adopted, immigrant, socially isolated, or financially vulnerable people are targeted at higher rates. A future ethical dataset could test that hypothesis instead of asserting it. Selection bias matters. Names of accused people should remain private; a public accusation database would contaminate testimony and create safety and defamation risks.
